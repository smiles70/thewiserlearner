# L-011 — Mitzner et al. (2010). Older Adults Talk Technology

## Why this is in the library

This is the **definitive qualitative complement** to the CREATE empirical paper
(L-004). The CREATE team interviewed 113 older adults (65–85) about their actual
technology use, the technology they like, the technology they dislike, and why —
in their own words.

The paper's headline finding refutes a deeply embedded stereotype:

> Older adults are **more positive than negative** about technology, and the
> positives outweigh the negatives across virtually every domain studied.

It is the single best source for grounding the contract's tone rules in the
**actual voice** of the audience rather than in design-team assumptions.

## Core claims used by the contract

1. **Net attitude is positive.** Across home, work, and healthcare technologies,
   older adults reported more positives than negatives.
2. **The positives are concrete and meaningful**: convenience, support for
   activities, enhanced quality of life, social connection, and feeling
   independent.
3. **The negatives are also concrete and instrumental**: design that doesn't fit
   needs, features that are inconvenient or unreliable, security/privacy concerns,
   and frustration with poor onboarding — *not* "fear" or "stubbornness."
4. **Older adults reason about technology in cost-benefit terms**, the same way
   younger adults do. They adopt when the value clears the cost, and they reject
   when it does not — rationally.
5. **They want to be taught**, but they want to be taught well: at their pace, with
   a real reason, by someone who treats them as capable adults.

## How the contract should use this source

- Cite as the **primary warrant** for the contract's tone rules: respectful,
  cost-benefit-honest, never patronising, never warning-laden.
- Cite when defending the rule that every video opens by **naming a concrete benefit
  in the viewer's own life context** (independence, family connection, time saved)
  rather than abstract claims about "AI."
- Cite when defending the rule against framing older adults as "afraid of"
  technology — Mitzner et al. is the empirical refutation of that frame.
- Cite when defending the contract's preference for **showing real older adults
  using real tools** rather than abstract iconography.
- Cite when justifying that **honesty about limitations** ("this part is genuinely
  inconvenient; here's the workaround") builds trust faster than smoothing them
  over.
