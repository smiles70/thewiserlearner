# L-012 — Hauk, Hüffmeier & Krumm (2018). Meta-analysis of Age and Technology Acceptance

## Why this is in the library

This is the **highest evidence-tier reference** in the technology-acceptance
literature for the contract: a meta-analysis pooling effect sizes across a large
set of primary studies on the relationship between chronological age and the
constructs of TAM/UTAUT (perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use,
behavioural intention, facilitating conditions, social influence, etc.).

In a library whose discipline is "find at least three references then proceed,"
meta-analyses are gold. This one underwrites every age-and-acceptance claim the
contract will need to make.

## Core claims used by the contract

1. **Age effects on acceptance constructs are smaller and more variable than the
   stereotype implies.** Pooled effect sizes for age on perceived usefulness,
   perceived ease of use, and behavioural intention are small to moderate, with
   substantial heterogeneity across studies.
2. **The moderators are predictable and modifiable.** Effect sizes vary with
   technology type, voluntariness of use, and study design — not with
   immutable older-adult characteristics.
3. **Effort expectancy (ease of use) is the construct where age matters most**,
   and it is precisely the construct most under designer control.
4. **Social influence matters more for older adults than for younger adults in
   several studies.** Family members, peers, and trusted media voices are
   disproportionately influential at older ages.
5. The findings are **broadly consistent with STAM** (L-009, L-010): the gap is
   in design and facilitating conditions, not in the older adult.

## How the contract should use this source

- Cite as the **strongest-tier evidence** anywhere the contract needs to assert
  that "age is not the dominant predictor of acceptance." A meta-analysis is the
  cleanest possible warrant.
- Cite when defending the channel's role as a **trusted social-influence voice**
  (the meta-analysis confirms this lever exists and is large).
- Cite when defending the contract's focus on **effort expectancy** (ease of use)
  as the highest-yield design target.
- Cite in the contract's evidence-grading rubric as the example of "Tier A"
  evidence: meta-analytic, multi-study, peer-reviewed.
