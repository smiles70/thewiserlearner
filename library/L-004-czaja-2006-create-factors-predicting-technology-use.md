# L-004 — Czaja et al. (2006). CREATE: Factors Predicting Technology Use

## Why this is in the library

This is the **flagship empirical study** of the Center for Research and Education on
Aging and Technology Enhancement (CREATE) — a multi-site NIA-funded program that
remains the most cited empirical evidence base for designing digital technology with
and for older adults.

The paper reports a structural-equation analysis of 1,204 community-dwelling adults
aged 18–91 and identifies **which variables actually predict technology use** in
later life. Its conclusions are the empirical backbone of every subsequent
"design for older adults" handbook — including the Fisk/Czaja/Rogers/Charness/Sharit
volumes and the third-edition Czaja/Boot/Charness/Rogers handbook (see L-007).

## Core claims used by the contract

1. **Age is not the dominant predictor.** When cognitive abilities (especially fluid
   intelligence and processing speed), computer self-efficacy, and computer anxiety
   are entered into the model, the direct effect of chronological age becomes small.
   Most of age's apparent effect on technology use is **mediated** by these variables.
2. **Computer self-efficacy and low computer anxiety are the largest practical
   levers.** They are modifiable through design and instruction; chronological age is
   not.
3. **Cognitive abilities matter** — fluid intelligence and crystallized intelligence
   both contribute, but in different ways. Implication: pacing, chunking, and
   working-memory-friendly interface choices are not "nice to have" — they
   measurably change use.
4. **Experience effects are real but bounded.** Prior experience predicts use but
   does not eliminate the cognitive-ability effects.

## How the contract should use this source

- Cite to **forbid age-as-deficit framing**. The contract states the audience is "older
  adults" because that is who watches; it does **not** assume reduced ability. Czaja
  et al. is the empirical warrant for that stance.
- Cite when defending **anxiety-reduction** language and pacing in scripts (slow,
  reassuring, no time pressure, errors normalised). The largest design lever
  identified by CREATE is anxiety reduction, not simplification.
- Cite when defending **self-efficacy-building** structure (small wins early,
  named successes, step-by-step proof that the viewer can do this).
- Cite when justifying **cognitive-load discipline** (one concept per video,
  ≤120 wpm, on-screen anchors).
